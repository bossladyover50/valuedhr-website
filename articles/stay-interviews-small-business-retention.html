Stay Interviews: The Retention Tool Most Small Businesses Skip

By Michelle Mendez  •  August 7, 2026  •  5 min read
← Back to Blog

Most small businesses only find out why a good employee left after they've already handed in notice. By then, the decision is made and the exit interview — if it happens at all — is really just a debrief. A stay interview flips the timing: it's a short, structured conversation with a current employee, held while they're still engaged, aimed at surfacing what's working, what isn't, and what might eventually push them out the door.

Why This Deserves a Slot on Your Calendar

Gallup's long-running research on voluntary turnover has consistently found that a majority of employees who quit say their manager or company could have done something to keep them — the departure wasn't inevitable, it just went unaddressed. Separately, current labor-market data gives you the backdrop: the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics' Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (JOLTS) tracks the national quits rate month to month, and industry retention research puts a meaningful share of turnover in the "preventable" category — driven by things like unclear growth paths, manager relationships, and unaddressed frustrations rather than pay alone.

Small businesses actually have an advantage here that larger companies can't easily replicate: fewer layers between leadership and the front line, and enough visibility into day-to-day work to catch problems early — if someone is asking the right questions.

What a Stay Interview Actually Is

It's not a performance review, and it's not a survey. It's a 15- to 30-minute one-on-one with a current, performing employee, held on a regular cadence (twice a year works well for most small teams), with one goal: understand what keeps them here and what could eventually change that.

The manager's job in a stay interview is to talk 20% of the time and listen for 80%. Ask, listen, and only ask the next question once you've actually absorbed the answer.

Five Questions That Do the Work

SHRM-published research from retention consultant Richard Finnegan identifies five core questions that consistently surface useful information. You don't need more than these — the skill is in the follow-up probes, not the question count.

  • What do you look forward to each day when you come to work? Grounds the conversation in the daily reality of the job, not abstractions.
  • What are you learning here, and what do you want to learn? Surfaces development and growth expectations before they become a reason to leave.
  • Why do you stay here? Most people have never had to articulate this — making them say it out loud clarifies it for both of you.
  • When's the last time you thought about leaving, and what prompted it? A direct question that brings a normally private thought into the open.
  • What can I do to make your job better for you? Puts the manager's own performance on the table — and requires taking the answer without getting defensive.

What to Do With What You Hear

A stay interview that produces notes nobody acts on is worse than not asking at all — it signals that feedback goes nowhere. Build a habit of turning takeaways into small, visible changes: a shifted responsibility, a skill-building opportunity, a scheduling adjustment. If the same theme shows up across multiple conversations — a process, a policy, a manager pattern — treat that as a signal worth escalating, not a coincidence.

Trust builds when people see their input lead somewhere. That's what separates a stay interview program that improves retention from one that just becomes another box to check.
